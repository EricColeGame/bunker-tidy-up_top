## The Core Loop: Scan, Sort, Review

Every tidy session follows the same three beats, whether you are clearing one shelf or an entire storage room.

- **Scan:** Identify what is actually in front of you before you touch anything. Loose batteries, empty shelf slots, and items that clearly belong elsewhere are your three targets.
- **Sort:** Group like with like. Keep temporary piles narrow and out of walkways so you do not create a second mess while fixing the first.
- **Review:** Recheck the finished section before walking away. Ten seconds of checking saves minutes of backtracking later.

The temptation for new players is to grab the nearest battery and start placing it. That feels productive, but it usually scatters your attention across three or four half-finished areas. Observation first, movement second — that order is the whole trick.

| Loop phase | What you do | What it prevents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scan | Survey one area fully before touching anything | Grabbing the first item you see |
| Sort | Group similar batteries into one small holding spot | Mixed piles that are hard to audit |
| Review | Recheck the finished shelf before leaving | Misplaced items and emptied-but-unfilled slots |

One more rule worth internalizing early: if a shelf is already organized, leave it alone. Re-sorting clean shelves is the most common way beginners burn a whole session without measurable progress.

## Set Up Your First Sorting Route

A route is simply a fixed order of movement. It should start at a landmark you recognize instantly — a shelf edge, a table, a doorway — and flow through connected storage in one direction. Repeat the same route every session and navigation stops costing you attention.

Here is a first-session sequence that works for almost any bunker layout:

1. **Pick a starting landmark.** Choose the most visible shelf or table, not the crowded corner where several item groups overlap.
2. **Survey before moving.** Sweep your eyes across the whole section. Note loose batteries, empty space, and objects that clearly belong somewhere else.
3. **Make one temporary group.** Place the batteries you are handling in a single defined spot, close enough to reach but separate from finished storage.
4. **Finish the local shelf.** Complete the section you started before moving on. A finished shelf is a progress marker you can trust.
5. **Review and note the status.** Check the shelf again, decide whether it is complete, and start your next session at the nearest unfinished area.

| Route phase | Action | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Survey | Inspect the entire section | Picking items up immediately |
| Grouping | Keep exactly one temporary pile | Creating several mixed piles |
| Placement | Finish the chosen shelf | Leaving a half-sorted shelf behind |
| Review | Recheck the completed area | Assuming every item landed correctly |
| Transition | Move to the nearest unfinished section | Jumping randomly across the bunker |

Routes do not need to be perfect on day one. They need to be identical every time you return. Community reports from players juggling similar organizing tasks consistently mention that a fixed starting point matters more than an efficient one.

## A Battery Organization System Built for Scale

When the long-term goal involves hundreds of batteries, categorization has to be simple enough to use while tired. Broad groups beat precise ones early on; you can add detail once the basics feel automatic.

Use four working groups:

- **Unsorted:** Batteries found during the current scan. Keep this group small and temporary.
- **Ready to place:** Items you have confirmed and are about to store.
- **Finished:** Batteries already placed on the chosen shelf. Do not disturb them without a reason.
- **Review:** Anything whose category or destination is unclear. Resolve these before logging an area complete.

| Group | Purpose | Session rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unsorted | Newly found batteries | Empty it before switching sections |
| Ready to place | Confirmed items awaiting a slot | Keep it next to the active shelf |
| Finished | Already organized batteries | Leave untouched unless something is wrong |
| Review | Uncertain or misplaced items | Clear it before marking the area done |

The moment you merge an uncertain battery into a confirmed pile, that pile stops being trustworthy. A separate review group costs you one extra step and saves an entire re-sort later.

Player experience also suggests using visual landmarks inside the storage system itself — a shelf edge, a corner, a particular crate — so that similar-looking rooms do not blur together in your memory.

## Tracking Progress Without Repeating Areas

Large objectives become manageable the moment each area has a status. You do not need a spreadsheet; three states are enough.

- **Not started:** The area has never had a full scan.
- **In progress:** Items have been moved, but the shelf still needs a final review.
- **Complete:** The area was checked and holds no unresolved battery group.

| Status | Meaning | What to do next session |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Not started | No full scan yet | Survey the area first |
| In progress | Sorting began, review outstanding | Finish that same area |
| Complete | Checked and organized | Move to the nearest unfinished section |
| Review needed | One or more items remain uncertain | Resolve the review group |

Before you stop playing, run this short checklist:

- One starting shelf or storage section chosen
- Area surveyed before anything was moved
- One temporary battery group created and emptied
- Selected shelf finished and reviewed
- Area marked as not started, in progress, or complete

A short session is never wasted progress. Finishing one section cleanly reduces the number of areas competing for your attention later, which is exactly what makes a 420-battery goal feel reachable instead of overwhelming.

## Habits That Separate Smooth Sessions From Messy Ones

Once the route feels natural, the next improvement is not faster movement — it is less wasted movement. Keep your starting point consistent, finish local groups before changing rooms, and always park uncertain items in the review group instead of guessing.

| Habit | Beginner benefit | When to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One-section sessions | Progress stays easy to measure | Every session |
| Temporary review group | Prevents doubtful placements | Whenever an item is unclear |
| Fixed starting landmark | Routes stay memorable | Every return to the bunker |
| End-of-session audit | Cuts repeated searching | Right before you stop |
| Broad categories first | Keeps early sorting light | First several sessions |

## Bunker Tidy Up Beginner Guide FAQ

**What should a beginner focus on first?**

One visible shelf or storage section. Survey it before moving anything, group the batteries you find, finish that local area, and review it before widening your route. Following that order is the fastest way to make the rest of this bunker tidy up beginner guide feel intuitive.

**How many batteries are tied to the public organizing goal?**

Publicly shared game information highlights 420 batteries as a major collection and organization objective. Treat that as a long-term target and break it into finished sections rather than chasing the full number in one sitting.

**Should I sort every item type at once?**

No. Start with batteries and use broad groups — unsorted, ready to place, finished, and review. Add finer categories only after the basic system runs without thinking.

**How do I avoid cleaning the same area twice?**

Use a fixed starting landmark and label every area as not started, in progress, or complete. Ending each session with a quick review tells you exactly where the next one begins.

The dependable beginner approach never changes: scan first, sort one category, finish one section, review before moving on. With a consistent route, a cluttered bunker turns into a readable space and a huge battery count becomes a series of small, satisfying wins.
